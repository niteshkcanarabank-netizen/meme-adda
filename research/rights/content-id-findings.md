# Meme Adda — Formats That Travel + Content ID / Copyright Facts

**Research leg:** formats and rights enforcement  
**Platforms:** YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels and Facebook  
**Market:** Indian / Hinglish meme content  
**Date researched:** 23 October 2026  
**Status:** foundation research only — no production recommendation can override the rights findings below

## Executive summary

The format with the clearest Indian/Hinglish evidence is not simply “post a famous film scene”. It is a **short, culturally recognisable setup followed by an immediate comic payoff**. That payoff may be a film reaction, a second unrelated clip, an expectation-versus-reality cut, a POV performance, or — most safely — the creator’s own acted reaction or re-enactment.

The **two-clip “connect the dots” format is real and widespread**: an ordinary creator-shot situation or caption creates the setup, then a well-known reaction clip supplies the punchline. The juxtaposition is the joke. It is also the format with the worst rights profile when the second clip is downloaded from a film, serial, broadcast or another creator and edited externally. On YouTube it can produce a Content ID claim, a block, a takedown or a channel-level reused-content problem. On Meta it can trigger Rights Manager/copyright action and, even without a copyright removal, Meta’s originality systems can reduce distribution or monetisation because “stitching multiple clips together” is expressly treated as unoriginal when there is no meaningful new contribution.

The strongest observed engagement in this leg’s small social sample came not from raw clip insertion but from **original multi-role re-enactments of familiar comic situations**. That points to the practical Meme Adda adaptation: borrow the *comic grammar* — timing, contrast, reaction, recognisable situation — but make the published performance, script, footage and sound original or properly licensed.

### The one-sentence rights rule

**There is no verified “under X seconds is safe” rule for TV or film clips on YouTube, Instagram or Facebook.** If protected audio or video matches an enrolled reference, the rights holder’s policy decides what happens; if the use is externally edited and uploaded, calling it a meme, parody, reaction or fair use does not prevent a claim or takedown.

# Part 1 — Formats that travel

## 1. Evidence base and its limits

This leg used three kinds of evidence:

1. **Platform evidence:** YouTube’s official explanation of Shorts discovery.
2. **Observed social sample:** a query-led sample of 50 Meta posts/Reels retrieved on 23 October 2026. It showed formats in circulation and visible likes/comments, but generally did **not** expose views, shares, completion rate or revenue. It was query-biased and therefore proves circulation, not a reliable performance rate.
3. **Research literature:** an Indian meme-marketing study, a peer-reviewed study of 2 million Reddit visual memes, and a secondary summary of Publicis Groupe/TikTok trend research.

That distinction matters. Several creator-marketing pages found during searching made precise claims — for example, that POV or Hinglish formats produce a particular percentage uplift — without an inspectable dataset. Those claims have not been used.

YouTube’s own Shorts guidance also warns against format folklore: the recommendation system does **not** favour a particular Shorts format. It personalises around viewer history and signals including whether viewers choose to watch or skip, how long they stay, percentage viewed, average view duration, likes and satisfaction surveys [Y-SHORTS-DISCOVERY]. A format travels only when its execution earns those viewer behaviours.

## 2. Format findings

### 2.1 Two-clip “connect the dots” — setup clip, reaction payoff

**Structure**

1. Clip A: an everyday situation, selfie, caption or short acted setup.
2. Hard cut.
3. Clip B: a famous reaction, dialogue or facial expression that comments on Clip A.
4. The viewer supplies the connection; no explanatory narration is needed.

In the Indian/Hinglish sample, *Hera Pheri* material — especially Baburao reactions — functioned as shared comic shorthand. Examples included:

- A friends/travel caption — “Tum loog itna ghumte ho, kafi ameer honge” — cutting to the *Hera Pheri* “Kon hum?” reaction; about 15,000 likes, with comments mainly tagging friends [S1].
- A selfie followed by a black card reading “my reaction:” and an angry Baburao clip; only about 25 likes [S2].
- A Hindi complaint that a friend no longer shares Reels, cutting to Baburao’s “Ye badi acchi baat kahi aapne”; about 20 likes [S3].
- A creator-delivered setup/joke, a cut to Baburao’s approving reaction, then a return to the creator; about 1,100 likes and 50 comments [S4].
- A couple’s selfie/setup text cutting to labelled film reactions and another laughing-man clip; about 14 likes [S5].

**Finding:** the grammar is widespread and instantly legible, but the sample does **not** show that inserting a famous reaction guarantees reach. Visible outcomes ranged from roughly 14 likes to 15,000 likes. The recognisable clip supplies compression and shared meaning; it does not replace a strong setup, timing or audience fit.

**Rights exposure:** highest when Clip B is an externally downloaded film/serial scene. See Part 2.

**Safer adaptation:** keep Clip A and make Clip B an original acted reaction, a licensed reaction, or an eligible in-platform remix where the platform provides that route. The joke remains in the juxtaposition, but the protected scene is no longer simply republished.

### 2.2 Single-clip reaction memes

**Structure:** one famous expression, dialogue or scene is reused as the entire emotional response to a caption.

This overlaps with the two-clip format, but the protected clip carries more of the finished post. The same Baburao lines appeared across unrelated domestic, friendship and social situations in the August–October 2026 sample. This supports the conclusion that a reaction can become a reusable template even when each individual post performs modestly.

**Finding:** single reaction clips are efficient because the audience already knows the emotional meaning. Their weakness is sameness: once the reaction is over-familiar, the caption/setup must do more work.

**Rights exposure:** very high for direct film/serial excerpts. A caption or meme label is not, by itself, the “significant original commentary” or substantive modification YouTube’s reused-content policy looks for [Y-REUSED].

### 2.3 Template memes — replication with local modification

A template is not one video; it is a reusable relationship between setup and payoff. The peer-reviewed study of **2 million Reddit visual memes from 2011–2020** found that visual memes spread through replication with modification and that persistence was strongly associated with early adoption [R-REDDIT]. That study is not Shorts/Reels-specific, so it should not be converted into a Reels performance claim. It is nevertheless useful lifecycle evidence: templates survive when many creators can quickly produce recognisable variants, not when one canonical copy is endlessly reposted.

For Meme Adda, the durable asset is therefore the **format grammar**:

- relatable Indian household/work/friendship setup;
- Hinglish caption in the audience’s spoken register;
- immediate contrast or reaction;
- a repeatable ending gesture, phrase or character.

The specific film line or sound can rotate while the grammar remains.

An Indian meme-marketing study indexed as IJIRT204995 analysed ten Indian brand campaigns through content analysis and a consumer survey. It found Hinglish to be the predominant register in that sample and associated culturally congruent memes — Hinglish, recognisable formats and community-aligned humour — with stronger recall, brand-personality attribution and engagement. It also found reactive memes published within 24 hours of an event outperformed scheduled campaign memes on organic reach/resharing in that study [R-INDIA]. Caveat: these were brand campaigns, not independent meme pages.

### 2.4 POV text-overlay sketches

**Structure:** “POV: …” establishes the situation in the first frame; the creator performs the contrast or punchline.

Observed examples:

- A Facebook Reel contrasting “POV: Other Parents” with “POV: Your Parents”: polished/supportive behaviour first, chaotic punishment gag second; about 1,700 likes and comments such as “Reel ❌ Reality ✅”, with viewers tagging friends [S6].
- An Instagram POV office-crush performance with about 21,000 likes and 580 comments; the response centred on relatability and facial timing rather than plot [S7].
- A Facebook POV “desi mom” monologue with about 1,500 likes but little substantive comment activity [S8].

**Finding:** POV is a strong native grammar for original Meme Adda production because the premise is readable before any dialogue and the entire performance can be owned. It also travels without requiring a protected payoff clip. Performance still varies widely; “POV” is a framing device, not a reach guarantee.

**Rights exposure:** low when script, performance, footage and music are original or licensed.

### 2.5 Original sketches and re-enactments

The best visible engagement in this leg’s sample came from original performances built around familiar comic material:

- A 22 October 2026 Reel by Zahara Sethjiwala recreated a *Phir Hera Pheri* confrontation with the creator playing multiple roles; about 96,000 likes and 500 comments. Commenters quoted lines and praised the multi-role acting/editing [S9].
- A 14 October 2026 Reel by the same creator used an original car sketch built around the *Hera Pheri* “kidney mein heart attack” situation; about 220,000 likes and 1,200 comments. Viewers praised one performer playing all roles and asked how it was shot [S10].

**Finding:** in this limited sample, audiences rewarded visible original performance more than simple clip insertion. This is directional evidence, not a controlled comparison: account size, posting history, shares and views were not available.

**Rights exposure:** materially lower Content ID matching risk than publishing the original scene, because the published audiovisual recording is new. It is not automatically risk-free: closely copying protected dialogue, scripts, music, distinctive production elements or branding can still create copyright or other IP issues. The safer version uses a fresh script and performance inspired by a familiar *situation*, rather than reproducing the scene line-for-line.

### 2.6 Expectation versus reality

**Structure:** glamorous or idealised Clip A, followed by ordinary real-life Clip B.

Observed August 2026 examples used Bollywood-style romantic two-wheeler imagery followed by ordinary creator-shot footage of young men and the Hinglish shrug “Khair” [S11] [S12]. Their visible likes were low, so the examples establish circulation rather than breakout performance.

**Finding:** the contrast is durable because it needs no franchise-specific knowledge: status/fantasy first, ordinary Indian reality second. It can be produced entirely with original footage by reversing the usual rights problem — shoot both the “expectation” parody and the “reality” payoff instead of taking the expectation from a film.

**Rights exposure:** low if both sides are original/licensed; high if the glamorous half is a film clip.

### 2.7 Sound-led and event-led formats

A secondary summary of Publicis Groupe/TikTok research reported analysis of more than 1,000 leading trends across seven countries over six months [R-TREND-SECONDARY]. It reported:

- only 27% of trends survived beyond two weeks;
- nearly half disappeared within five days;
- sound-led trends lasted roughly 3–7 days, with a useful posting window around days 2–5;
- format-led trends lasted roughly 2–4 weeks;
- event/cultural trends peaked within roughly 1–2 weeks.

**Verification label:** these figures are **secondary**. The primary Publicis report was not directly inspected in this leg. They are useful planning ranges, not platform-official facts and not India-specific performance guarantees.

The practical distinction is between three lifespans:

| Layer | What dies or survives | Working implication |
|---|---|---|
| Exact sound/dialogue | Usually the fastest-moving layer | Use while current; do not build the page identity around it |
| Specific template/clip pairing | Can circulate for weeks, but becomes stale through repetition | Rotate setups and payoffs |
| Comic grammar | Setup → contrast/reaction can persist for years | Build repeatable original characters and structures around it |

The repeated use of Baburao reactions from August to October 2026 supports the third point: the *reaction grammar* persisted even though individual posts performed very differently.

## 3. Format verdict

| Format | Travel evidence | Main strength | Rights risk | Meme Adda verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Two-clip connect-the-dots with downloaded film/serial payoff | Widespread in sample; outcomes varied from ~14 to ~15K likes | Instant shared meaning; very fast joke | **High** | Do not build the foundation on externally edited protected payoffs |
| Two-clip structure with original/licensed payoff | Same grammar; performance depends on execution | Keeps the joke while owning the payoff | Low–medium | Preferred adaptation |
| Single famous reaction clip + caption | Repeatedly observed | Compression and recognition | **High** | Occasional licensed/remix use only; not a safe default |

# Part 2 — Content ID, copyright claims and originality: the verified picture

## 4. Four risks that folklore wrongly merges

A meme page faces four separate risks:

1. **Copyright claim:** the rights holder’s automated/manual claim applies a block, monetise or track policy.
2. **Copyright takedown/strike:** a formal removal request removes content and can produce a strike.
3. **Platform originality/monetisation enforcement:** YouTube’s reused-content policy or Meta’s unoriginal/limited-originality rules can affect the whole channel/Page even where no copyright takedown occurs.
4. **Copyright liability outside the platform systems:** platform non-enforcement is not permission and does not prove a legal defence.

A post can survive one system and fail another. In particular, “it only got a claim, not a strike” does not mean it is monetisable, recommendable or legally cleared.

## 5. YouTube Content ID: claim versus strike

### 5.1 What Content ID does

YouTube says Content ID compares uploads against audio and visual reference files supplied by copyright owners. When a match is found, the upload receives a Content ID claim and the owner’s chosen policy is applied [Y-CID]:

- **Block** the video from being viewed;
- **Monetise** it by running ads, sometimes sharing revenue with the uploader; or
- **Track** its viewing statistics.

Any of those actions can be territory-specific. The same video may be monetised in one country, blocked in another and tracked in a third [Y-CID] [Y-CLAIMS].

Content ID is limited to approved copyright owners who own exclusive rights to a substantial body of original material that is frequently uploaded to YouTube [Y-CID]. YouTube’s Content ID eligibility guidance expressly treats **television episodes** and **movies** as reference-asset types and requires identifying metadata for them [Y-CID-ELIGIBILITY]. That is directly relevant to Indian serials and Bollywood films: the system is designed for exactly these catalogue categories.

### 5.2 A claim is not a strike

YouTube states that a Content ID claim is not a copyright strike and normally does not affect the channel or account [Y-CLAIMS].

That does **not** mean repeated claims are strategically harmless:

- each claimed video can lose visibility or revenue according to the claimant’s policy;
- a disputed claim can escalate if the claimant responds with a formal copyright removal request;
- a channel built mainly from other people’s material can separately fail YouTube Partner Programme reused-content review;
- claims provide evidence that the channel’s production model depends on protected catalogue material.

**Verified bottom line:** claims do **not** accumulate as “three claims = channel terminated”. There is no official claim-count strike ladder in the sources reviewed. Channel termination risk comes from formal takedowns/strikes, programme-policy enforcement or other policy action — not from the number of ordinary Content ID claims alone.

### 5.3 What a copyright strike does

A copyright strike follows a valid legal copyright removal request [Y-STRIKES]. YouTube’s strike guidance provides that:

- a strike can be resolved by expiry after 90 days where the conditions are met, including completion of Copyright School;
- it can also be resolved by retraction or a valid counter-notification;
- **three copyright strikes within 90 days** expose the account and associated channels to termination, make uploaded content inaccessible and prevent creation of new channels [Y-STRIKES].

A creator should therefore treat a dispute as a legal escalation decision, not as a routine “appeal button”. YouTube warns that disputing a claim without a valid reason can lead the claimant to submit a copyright removal request; if valid, the video is removed and the channel receives a strike [Y-CLAIMS].

Valid dispute grounds are matters such as owning the necessary rights, holding a licence that actually covers the use, misidentification, public-domain status, or a genuinely applicable copyright exception. “It is a meme”, “I credited the owner”, “I am not monetising it” and “the clip is short” are not stand-alone dispute grounds.

## 6. Shorts-specific consequences

### 6.1 Shorts over one minute: any active claim blocks the Short

YouTube’s three-minute Shorts guidance states that a Short longer than one minute with **any active copyright claim**, including a manual claim, is blocked globally, is not playable or recommended and is not eligible for monetisation [Y-SHORTS-3MIN].

There is no channel penalty merely because that block occurs. Removing the claimed content or successfully resolving/disputing the claim can restore visibility and monetisation eligibility [Y-SHORTS-3MIN].

For Shorts of **1–3 minutes**, YouTube’s claims guidance is stricter than the ordinary claimant-policy model: any active claim blocks the Short regardless of the claimant’s usual policy [Y-CLAIMS].

### 6.2 Shorts under one minute

YouTube states that the new limitations for claimed 1–3 minute Shorts do not apply in the same way to Shorts under one minute [Y-SHORTS-3MIN]. A sub-one-minute Short therefore remains subject to the claimant’s ordinary block/monetise/track policy and territory settings.

That is not a safety rule. It means:

- **Block policy:** the Short can be blocked in some territories or more broadly according to the owner’s settings.
- **Monetise policy:** ads/revenue treatment follows the claim and Shorts revenue rules.
- **Track policy:** the Short can remain visible while the owner receives viewing statistics.

### 6.3 Shorts revenue is not the same as long-form claim revenue

Shorts advertising revenue is pooled and allocated through the Shorts Creator Pool. YouTube’s Shorts monetisation guidance says that, for calculating the pool, **only music content** supplied by YouTube music partners or Dream Track is currently credited as contributing third-party content; other third-party content categories are not currently credited in that calculation, even where a Content ID monetise policy exists [Y-SHORTS-MONETISATION].

The practical conclusion is narrower than common folklore:

- Do **not** say every visual film/TV claim on a Short simply “takes all the Short’s revenue” in the same way as a long-form monetise claim.
- Do say that a visual claim can block the Short, restrict territories, affect monetisation eligibility — and, for a Short over one minute, block it globally.
- Music is the clearest Shorts revenue-diversion category because YouTube expressly builds music licensing/partner contributions into the Creator Pool calculation.

For ordinary claimed videos, YouTube’s claim policy remains block/monetise/track, with monetisation sometimes shared with the uploader [Y-CLAIMS]. The exact financial result depends on video type, claimant, territory and programme status.

### 6.4 Shorts audio libraries

YouTube says songs in the Shorts Audio Library can generally be used for up to 90 seconds in a three-minute Short, but some songs are limited to 60 or 30 seconds [Y-SHORTS-3MIN]. It also states that royalty-free music from the YouTube Audio Library will not receive a Content ID claim [Y-SHORTS-3MIN].

Those permissions are platform- and product-specific. A song available inside YouTube Shorts is not thereby cleared for the same edit on Instagram, Facebook, or a later long-form repost.

## 7. TV and Bollywood clips: claim likelihood

### 7.1 What can and cannot be quantified

No official or primary source retrieved for this leg publishes a title-by-title probability that a particular Indian serial scene, Bollywood scene or song will be claimed. Any percentage assigned to a named programme or studio would be invented.

The supportable assessment is exposure-based:

- **Detection exposure is high for major catalogue material.** Content ID is built for owners with substantial, frequently uploaded catalogues; television episodes and movies are explicit reference types [Y-CID] [Y-CID-ELIGIBILITY].
- **The result is policy-dependent.** Detection does not dictate one outcome: the owner may block, monetise or track, by territory [Y-CID].
- **Short excerpts are not exempt from matching.** YouTube describes claims as arising when an upload matches another video “or segment of a video” [Y-CLAIMS]. No official minimum safe duration was found.
- **Manual claiming and formal takedowns remain available** where automated matching misses a use or a rights holder chooses escalation. YouTube’s copyright guidance distinguishes Content ID claims from copyright removal requests [Y-COPYRIGHT-TIPS].

### 7.2 Direct Indian evidence

Yash Raj Films’ own FAQ states: “As a policy, we do not permit any third party publishers to publish YRF copyrighted content on YouTube or any other video based platform,” and advises readers to refrain from using YRF copyrighted content “to avoid any legal implications” [I-YRF]. That is a primary-source warning against treating YRF scenes as informally available for meme publication.

A 2020 Gadgets 360 report gives a real Indian creator example: YouTube confirmed that T-Series had made a Content ID claim on creator Salil Jamdar’s video and that the claim blocked it for viewers, without becoming a strike or takedown; T-Series later manually resolved the claims and Jamdar made the video private [I-GADGETS360]. The same report described Indian parody creators losing advertising revenue on claimed videos and YRF’s earlier refusal to permit All India Bakchod to parody the *Dhoom 3* trailer. This is reputable secondary evidence and a useful illustration, but it is dated and anecdotal; it does **not** establish a current claim rate for T-Series, YRF or Bollywood catalogues generally.

### 7.3 Risk tiers for Indian TV/film material

These are operational risk tiers, not measured probabilities:

| Material | Claim / enforcement exposure | Reason |
|---|---:|---|
| Current or highly popular Bollywood film scenes and songs | **Very high** | Large, valuable catalogues; specialist rights owners; explicit movie/music reference categories |
| Major-network Indian serial scenes and dialogues | **Very high** | Television episodes are explicit Content ID assets; networks/broadcasters hold substantial frequently reused catalogues |
| News, sports and broadcaster clips | **Very high** | Broadcast catalogues are reference material and may also be manually enforced |
| Older or less prominent film/TV scenes | **Variable, still material** | Depends on whether the specific work/territory is enrolled and the owner’s policy; age alone is not clearance |
| Very short, cropped, pitched or otherwise altered fragments | **Variable, not safe** | Alteration may affect matching, but there is no official safe duration or transformation threshold; manual claims/takedowns remain possible |
| A scene used only as background audio/video | **Material** | Matching can arise from a segment; “background” is not a rights category |
| Original re-enactment with new script/performance | **Lower matching exposure** | No original scene is republished, but protected dialogue/script/music can still be infringed if copied too closely |
| Licensed clip with written commercial rights for the exact platform/use | **Low if the licence is genuine and complete** | Licence must cover the clip, platform, territory, term, editing and monetisation |

### 7.4 Why “everyone posts these scenes” proves little

High volumes of infringing or claimed uploads can coexist with active enforcement because a rights holder may choose **track** or **monetise** rather than block. Survival of somebody else’s post can mean any of the following:

- the catalogue is not enrolled for that title/territory;
- the owner’s policy is track or monetise;
- the match was missed;
- the uploader had permission;
- enforcement has not yet occurred;
- the post was claimed but the claim is not visible to viewers.

It does not establish permission, fair dealing or a transferable safe harbour.

## 8. YouTube monetisation: reused content is a channel-level risk

Copyright and monetisation policy are separate. YouTube states that monetisable content must be original and non-repetitious, and the creator must own the necessary rights to commercially use all visual and audio elements [Y-MONETISE].

YouTube’s reused-content policy defines reused content as repurposing content from YouTube or another online source without significant original commentary, substantive modification, or educational/entertainment value. The policy applies to the **channel as a whole**; monetisation can be removed from the entire channel if reviewers cannot clearly tell that the channel made the content [Y-REUSED].

Examples YouTube says can be allowed include [Y-REUSED]:

- clips used for a critical review;
- a film scene with rewritten dialogue and changed voiceover;
- reaction videos where the creator comments on the original;
- edited footage from others with an added storyline and commentary.

Examples it says violate the policy include [Y-REUSED]:

- favourite-show clips edited together with little or no narrative;
- compilations of short videos from other social media;
- content whose views come mainly from non-verbal reactions without voice commentary.

This is the central problem for a pure two-clip page. The juxtaposition may be funny and culturally creative, but if the page’s value comes mainly from two protected clips and the creator adds little visible/audible original contribution, a YPP reviewer can still treat the channel as reused content. A caption alone is a weak contribution. A newly performed setup is stronger; substantial voice commentary, rewritten dialogue, original storyline and creator-led transformation are stronger still — but none converts an infringing clip into a licensed clip.

YouTube also states that recording material from TV, DVD or CD does not transfer the underlying rights, and commercial use of recorded material requires written permission from the rights owner of the recorded elements [Y-MONETISE]. Buying, streaming or finding material online likewise does not itself supply commercial-use rights.

## 9. YouTube Remix: what is actually permitted

YouTube’s official Remix tools are materially different from downloading a scene and assembling an MP4 externally. Eligible content can be used through tools including [Y-REMIX]:

- **Sound:** sample eligible audio;
- **Cut:** sample a 1–5-second segment of an eligible music video;
- **Green Screen:** use eligible video visually or audio-visually as a background;
- **Collab** and templates for eligible content.

Remixed Shorts are attributed to the source. Availability is not universal: privacy settings, ownership claims, creator/partner opt-outs and licence restrictions can make material unavailable [Y-REMIX].

YouTube expressly warns that a short video created elsewhere must use material approved for use on YouTube; protected material in an externally edited upload can receive a Content ID claim or a takedown/strike [Y-REMIX].

Remix eligibility is also not a permanent property right in the underlying work. If the source creator later restricts remixing or deletes the source, YouTube says audio remixes may be muted, unlisted and scheduled for deletion, while video remixes may be deleted [Y-REMIX].

**Operational distinction:**

- Building inside an eligible YouTube Remix flow = platform-provided route, subject to eligibility and later source changes.
- Downloading a serial/film scene, editing it in external software and uploading the result = ordinary upload for Content ID, takedown and reused-content purposes.

## 10. Fair use and Indian fair dealing: the myth versus the law

### 10.1 United States fair use

YouTube’s fair-use guidance states that, in US law, courts decide fair use case by case using four factors [Y-FAIRUSE]:

1. purpose and character of the use;
2. nature of the copyrighted work;
3. amount and substantiality taken;
4. effect on the potential market for or value of the work.

Adding new expression or meaning can help, but does not guarantee fair use. Attribution, “no infringement intended”, entertainment-only labelling and non-profit status do not automatically create protection. Even a small excerpt can weigh against fair use if it is the “heart” of the work. Content ID cannot decide fair use; only a court can make the legal determination [Y-FAIRUSE].

A reaction use is therefore not automatically fair use merely because it is funny, short, transformative in a loose everyday sense, or labelled “parody”. A use that actually criticises or reviews the copied scene is legally different from using the scene merely as a ready-made punchline for an unrelated situation.

### 10.2 Indian fair dealing

Section 52(1)(a) of India’s Copyright Act, 1957 covers fair dealing with a work for [I-ACT]:

- private or personal use, including research;
- criticism or review of that work or another work;
- reporting current events and current affairs.

The Copyright Office’s exceptions page reflects the same categories [I-EXCEPTIONS].

No general Indian “meme”, “entertainment” or “parody” safe harbour was verified in the official text reviewed for this leg. That matters because a public, commercial meme page is not obviously engaged in private use, criticism/review of the copied work, or reporting current events merely because it uses a famous funny scene as a reaction.

**Bottom line on fair dealing:** criticism/review is narrower than “the clip is being used comedically”. A scene used to comment on *that scene, performance or work* has a different argument from a scene used as a generic emotional reaction to an unrelated household joke. Neither platform automation nor a creator’s assertion decides the question.

## 11. Meta: Facebook and Instagram

### 11.1 Copyright and Rights Manager

Meta’s Help Centre states that Facebook and Instagram terms permit posting only content that does not violate another person’s intellectual-property rights. Meta’s safest stated rule is to post content you created yourself. Other content may be usable with permission/a licence, if it is public domain, or if a copyright exception applies [M-COPYRIGHT].

Meta’s Transparency Centre states that it removes or restricts content engaging in copyright infringement and removes accounts that repeatedly violate its IP policy. Authorised use and content following established fair-use principles are allowed [M-IP].

Rights Manager is Meta’s rights-holder system for controlling when, how and where protected content is shared on Facebook and Instagram. Meta describes a broader IP programme including proactive measures, a global notice-and-takedown team, specialised reporting tools and a repeat-infringer policy [M-RIGHTS].

Therefore, the absence of YouTube-style “Content ID claim” terminology on Meta does not mean lower legal safety. The enforcement vocabulary and workflow differ; the underlying permission problem remains.

### 11.2 Limited originality and unoriginal content

Meta’s Transparency Centre says it may reduce distribution on Facebook and Instagram for “limited originality content that is principally repurposed from other sources”, even where the content does not violate Community Standards [M-DISTRIBUTION].

Meta’s 13 March 2026 Newsroom post, “Rewarding Original Creators on Facebook”, gives the clearest operational definition [M-ORIGINAL]:

- Content filmed or produced directly by the creator/Page owner is original.
- A Reel selectively incorporating third-party material can be original when the focus is the creator on screen presenting something genuinely new — fresh information, analysis or a substantial improvement to a storyline.
- Merely watching along, reacting with facial expressions, **stitching multiple clips together**, or narrating what is already on screen without a meaningful addition is classified as unoriginal and likely to be deprioritised.
- Re-uploading another creator’s post or making minor edits such as borders, captions or speed changes is unoriginal and deprioritised.
- Creators who continue to post primarily unoriginal content may become non-recommendable and demonetised.

Meta’s 14 July 2025 announcement, as reported in English by TechCrunch, applies the same direction to Facebook monetisation: accounts repeatedly reusing other people’s material without meaningful enhancement can lose access to Facebook monetisation programmes for a period and receive reduced distribution across everything they share; duplicate videos receive reduced distribution so originals can gain visibility [M-UNORIGINAL-REPORT].

**Direct consequence for the two-clip format:** Meta does not merely ask, “Was there a copyright claim?” It also asks whether stitching the clips was a meaningful original contribution. A pure Clip A + Clip B post is therefore exposed on two independent fronts — copyright/Rights Manager and originality/distribution/monetisation.

### 11.3 Instagram aggregator recommendation risk — secondary evidence

TechCrunch’s report of Instagram’s 30 April 2024 ranking changes said accounts that post other users’ content without material enhancement 10 or more times in 30 days can be removed from recommendations [M-IG-REPORT]. The original Instagram announcement was not retrieved directly in this leg, so this threshold is labelled **secondary, not official-source-verified here**. It should be treated as a warning signal, not as a safe allowance of nine unoriginal posts.

### 11.4 What was not verified for Meta

- A commonly repeated claim that Meta imposes a specific **90-day limited-originality demonetisation period** was not verified from an accessible official Meta source in this leg. It should not be quoted as fact without a further official check.
- Meta Sound Collection/music-library terms were not separately verified in this leg. Do not assume every in-app sound is cleared for every commercial, branded or cross-platform use.

## 12. What is comparatively safe, what earns claims, and what risks the channel/Page

### Comparatively safe — subject to the exact licence/platform terms

- Original footage, script, performance, artwork and music created for Meme Adda.
- Original POV sketches and multi-role performances.
- Original re-enactments that do not reproduce protected dialogue, music or scenes line-for-line.
- Properly licensed material where the written licence covers the exact asset, editing, platform, territory, term and commercial/monetisation use.
- Verified public-domain material, with the underlying music, performance and recording layers checked separately.
- YouTube Audio Library royalty-free music used according to YouTube’s terms.
- Eligible YouTube Remix content created inside YouTube’s own tools, recognising that eligibility can change and the source can later be restricted or deleted.
- Platform-provided Shorts audio within its stated duration and product limits, on YouTube only.

“Comparatively safe” does not mean immune from false claims, later rights changes or disputes. It means the production does not begin by republishing somebody else’s protected scene.

### Likely to earn claims, blocks or removal

- Externally edited Bollywood film scenes, songs or trailers.
- Externally edited Indian TV-serial scenes/dialogues.
- Broadcaster, news and sports clips.
- Another creator’s Reel/Short/TikTok used as Clip B.
- Compilations of famous scenes with captions as the only addition.
- Background film/TV/music that matches a reference segment.
- A 1–3 minute Short containing any actively claimed material: YouTube says it will be blocked globally.
- Cropped, mirrored, pitched, sped-up or filtered versions of the same protected material: alteration is not a verified safe harbour and does not remove takedown risk.

### Risks the channel or Page itself

- **Three YouTube copyright strikes within 90 days:** account/associated-channel termination risk [Y-STRIKES].
- **Disputing weak claims:** claimant escalation to a formal removal request and strike [Y-CLAIMS].
- **YouTube reused-content enforcement:** loss of monetisation for the channel as a whole where the channel is mainly repurposed material without significant original contribution [Y-REUSED].
- **Meta repeat-infringer enforcement:** account removal for repeated IP violations [M-IP].
- **Meta unoriginal-content enforcement:** reduced distribution, non-recommendability and loss of monetisation for Pages/accounts built mainly on repurposed clips [M-ORIGINAL] [M-DISTRIBUTION].
- **Legal exposure outside the platforms:** a platform’s decision to track, monetise or leave a post up is not a licence and does not extinguish the rights holder’s legal claims.

## 13. Foundation implications for Meme Adda

These are research implications, not a production launch decision:

1. **Do not make downloaded TV/film payoffs the core asset.** That model creates claim, block, reused-content and Meta-originality exposure simultaneously.
2. **Keep the two-clip joke, replace the protected payoff.** Use an original acted reaction, a recurring Meme Adda character, or licensed/platform-eligible material.
3. **Build owned templates.** A repeatable setup grammar and original characters can travel without depending on a catalogue the page does not control.
4. **Treat any clip use as an exception requiring a rights note.** Record source, owner, licence/permission, platform, territory, term, editing rights and monetisation rights before use.
5. **Never use claim survival as clearance.** A previous post receiving only a track/monetise claim does not clear the next post or another platform.
6. **Separate YouTube and Meta clearance.** YouTube Remix or Shorts-audio eligibility does not clear the same edit for Instagram or Facebook.
7. **Avoid weak disputes.** Dispute only with documents or a specific, supportable exception; otherwise remove/replace the segment.
8. **Keep Shorts containing any third-party material under particular scrutiny.** If a Short exceeds one minute and has an active claim, YouTube blocks it globally.
